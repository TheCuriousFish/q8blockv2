The problem with buying search work is that you are buying something you cannot see and cannot judge yourself, usually from somebody using vocabulary designed to sound complicated. That is a very comfortable position for anyone who does not know the work.

You do not need a technical background to tell the two apart. You need seven questions, and you need to insist on a clear answer to each one.

## What will exist in a month that does not exist today

This is the most important question on the list. A good answer describes things you can point at: specific pages that will be written and published, specific technical faults that will be fixed, existing pages that will be rewritten. A bad answer describes effort rather than output. "Ongoing optimisation." "Regular monitoring." "Work on the keywords."

Effort is not a product. Ask what you will own at the end of the month.

## Will I see the data in my own account

You should have a Google Search Console account for your own domain, with the highest level of access held by you. That is Google's data about your website and it is yours by default.

Anybody who shows you his reports but does not give you access to the source has put himself between you and the truth. A nicely designed report is not data. It is a presentation of data, and the difference is large.

## Who owns the website, the domain and the content if we stop

Ask this on the first call, not the last. Who owns the domain name and whose name is it registered in? If the arrangement ends, what is left with you?

There is no single correct answer here, because arrangements differ. The correct answer is a clear one, in writing. The dangerous answer is evasion or a change of subject.

## Are you working on my current site or building a new one, and why

Either can be right, but there has to be a reason you can follow. If he says he will work on your current site, ask what about the current site allows that. If he says he will build a new one, ask what about the current site prevents building on it.

The technical foundation of a website decides what can be done with it later. Anybody who cannot describe the state of the foundation he is about to build on has not looked at it.

## Do you need access to my Google profile, and what for

Pay attention here. There is a large difference between manager access and ownership of the profile. A manager can edit. An owner can remove you.

Do not transfer ownership of your profile to anyone, whatever reason is given. If you grant manager access, the owner account must stay yours, in your name and on your email. That profile is a real asset of your business, and its reviews took years to build.

## What happens to the work already done if we stop

The pages that were written and the technical fixes stay on the site. But ask clearly about the things that stop when the payments stop: hosting, the domain, any shared third party tools. These are purely practical questions and the answers should be ready and plain.

## Show me a site you built and the months behind it

Ask to see a real site he can open in front of you, and to see what happened to it across consecutive months rather than one chosen month. A single month says nothing. A continuous run of months says everything, because it shows the dips as well as the climbs.

## Warning signs that should end the call

**A promise of a specific position in the search results.** Nobody owns that promise. Results depend on your sector and on your competitors, and both sit outside the control of whoever is selling to you.

**A promise with a date on it.** "Results in two months" is not a sentence anybody can honestly guarantee. We do not give a timeline ourselves, and we say so in writing in our FAQ.

**A promise of appearing in AI answers.** Nobody controls what an AI assistant says. What can honestly be offered is clear, indexed, quotable pages, and that is a different thing from a guarantee.

**"We have a relationship with Google."** No such relationship exists. It is a sentence said to somebody assumed not to know.

**Reports about effort instead of results.** Twenty pages on how many keywords were researched and how many changes were made, with no numbers from your own account, is a report about being busy rather than a report about work.

**Price as the only argument.** Cheap here usually means copied content and duplicated pages, and both do more harm than good.

## One last thing

If you come off the call not understanding what you are going to get, the problem is not you. People who know the work can explain it in ordinary words, because they actually understand it. Vagueness in this field is rarely a sign of depth. It is usually what a missing deliverable hides behind.
