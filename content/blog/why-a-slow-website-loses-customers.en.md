Picture the moment as it actually happens. Somebody has a problem he needs solved today. He picks up his phone, types the service and the area, and opens the first three results in three tabs. He does not read the first one and then the second. He opens them together and starts with whichever one is ready.

The slow website loses right there, before anybody has read a word of it, and before you have any idea something happened. There is no missed call, no message, no trace. It is the only loss in your business that shows up nowhere at all.

## What actually makes websites slow

The causes are repetitive to the point of being boring, and they are mostly the same on every slow site.

**Images.** This is the first cause and it is not close. A photo taken on a phone can be several megabytes. Uploaded as it is and displayed small on screen, the browser downloads the whole thing and then shrinks it. A page with ten of those is asking your customer to wait for files he will only ever see a fraction of.

**Accumulated plugins.** Sites built on ready made templates collect plugins over time: one for forms, one for the gallery, one for bookings, one for the cookie banner. Each one loads its own files on every page, including the pages that never use it.

**Third party tools.** The chat bubble, the analytics tool, the ad pixel, an embedded map, a font pulled from an outside service. Every one of them opens a connection to another server before your page is finished. Four of them is enough to turn a light page into a heavy one.

**Fonts loading before the text.** Some sites wait for the font file before they show a single letter. The customer is staring at a white screen while the text is already there, just hidden.

**Hosting.** A crowded shared server on another continent adds a delay before the page even begins to load. No amount of tuning the page itself fixes that.

## The embedded map, a clear example

The embedded map on a contact page is a good example because it is so common and the fix is so simple. The map loads a large amount of code every single time the page opens, whether the visitor looks at it or not. Most visitors do not look at it, because they came for the number.

The fix is to show a still placeholder where the map goes, and only load the real map when the visitor taps it. Whoever wants the map gets it, and whoever wants the number does not pay for the wait. That is exactly what the contact page on this site does.

## Speed is not a score in a testing tool

It is easy to get distracted by the score in a speed testing tool. The score is useful to whoever is fixing the site, but it is not what matters to you.

What matters to you is one question. How long until your customer can read what he came for and tap your number? A site with a middling score that shows the text and the number immediately is better for your business than a site with a high score that makes the visitor watch a blank screen for two seconds.

There is a second effect that is less visible. Search engines crawl your site with limited resources, and heavy slow pages do not work in your favour when it is their turn to be indexed.

## How to check your site without understanding code

Open your site on your own phone, on mobile data rather than the office wifi. Those are your customer's real conditions. Do it on an inner page and not only the homepage, because the homepage is usually the only one anybody bothered with.

If you are paying somebody for the site, here are three questions you can ask with no technical background. How big is the largest image on the page? How many third party tools load on every page, and do we need all of them? Does the writing appear before the font loads or after it? Clear answers to those three tell you more about who you are working with than any report full of coloured charts.

## Why we build the websites ourselves

Speed is not something added to a finished website at the end of the job. It is the result of decisions made in the first days of the build: how images are processed, how many outside tools are allowed onto the page, how the fonts load, and where the site is hosted. Whoever builds the site makes those decisions, and that is one of the reasons we only work on sites we build.
