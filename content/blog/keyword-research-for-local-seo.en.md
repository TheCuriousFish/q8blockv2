A cleaning company owner writes on his site "professional cleaning services with modern equipment," while his customer types into Google "apartment cleaning before handover in Hawally." Both are talking about roughly the same service, but in words that never meet. That gap is exactly what **keyword research** deals with: finding the sentence your customer actually types, not the sentence you use for your own trade. A keyword is not a complicated term. It is simply the sentence a person types into a search box before he opens a page or picks up the phone.

This work does not need technical skill or paid software. It needs you to look at your business through your customer's eyes, not through the eyes of someone who works in the trade.

## Your word is not your customer's word

Every tradesman speaks the language of his trade. You know the difference between types of cleaning, or between different AC faults if that is your field, and your customer does not know that difference and does not care about it. He knows his problem: an apartment he needs handed over clean, a stain on a carpet, an AC unit making a noise and not cooling. When he types into Google, he types his problem in his own words, not your trade's terms.

That means a page using only your own words might be professionally accurate, but it will never meet what the person searching for you actually types. The gap between the two words is exactly the gap between a website that exists and a call that never arrives.

## Where to find the real words for free

You do not need to buy any software to find these words. Four free sources are enough.

- **Google's autocomplete and related searches**: type the start of a general sentence into the search box and see what Google suggests to finish it, and check the "related searches" at the bottom of the results page too.
- **The questions you already get asked on the phone and on WhatsApp**: you already own this list, you just have not written it down yet. Ask whoever answers the phone at your business to note the first question every caller asks.
- **The wording on your competitors' pages**: not to copy it, but to notice the words they use for the same service, and the areas they name specifically.
- **The search terms report inside your Google Business Profile**: your profile shows the actual sentences people typed before they reached it, a source you already have without any extra work.

Collect these words into one simple list, even a plain notebook. The value is not in the tool. It is in seeing them all together in front of you.

## A buying search versus a browsing search

Not every sentence carries the same value. A search like "best cleaning companies" is usually a browsing search, someone comparing with no decision close by. A search like "villa cleaning company in Jahra today" is a buying search, someone who wants to call soon. The difference usually shows up in the detail: the more detail a searcher adds about the service, the place and the timing, the closer he is to calling.

That does not mean ignoring the browsing search entirely, since it also brings people who read and remember your name for later. But it means your main pages, and the bulk of the effort spent writing them, should go to the sentences closest to a decision, because they are the ones closest to an actual call.

## Why "service plus area" phrasing matters

A local customer searches in an almost fixed pattern: the name of the service, then the name of his area. That is not an accident. He wants to be sure that whoever shows up actually serves his place, not somewhere else in the city. A page carrying that pattern in its heading answers two questions at once: do you offer this service, and do you reach this place.

A general page that only names the service without an area misses half the question, and a general line like "we serve all areas of Kuwait" reassures nobody, because every site writes it. The name of the actual area, written in the heading rather than buried in a paragraph, is what makes a customer stop searching.

## Do not chase one broad word

A common mistake is for an owner to put all his effort into one broad word like "cleaning Kuwait," assuming it is the word that brings everything. Every business in the same field competes for that word regardless of size, and it is usually a browsing search rather than a buying one, because it carries no detail about the searcher's place or actual need.

Dozens of specific sentences, each with less competition and a clearer intent, bring in more calls than one broad word ever will. Time spent chasing one word is better spent covering dozens of real questions instead.

## From the list to a plan: one page per real question

Once you have gathered words from the four sources, sort them into two columns: your real services, and the areas you actually reach. Every crossing point between a service and an area is a real question that deserves a page answering it alone.

Do not try to write all of it at once. Start with the crossings you hear most often on the phone, because they are the closest to a customer's decision, then work through the rest gradually. The list itself keeps changing as you hear new questions, so add each one the moment you hear it rather than trying to remember it later.
